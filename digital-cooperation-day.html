Chris Meniw

Governance of agentic AI · Founder, Chris Meniw Foundation · Buenos Aires, Argentina

I work on the governance of agentic AI: the standards, identity and accountability layers that decide how autonomous agents act for people and institutions. A lawyer and university professor for 16 years, I now build and release that layer as open, DOI-registered public goods — free to adopt, in any country, without permission or licence fees.

Speaking sample — English translation

Translated from the broadcast audio. Interviewer questions are summarised in brackets; all other text is Chris Meniw speaking.

00:48 [On launching the first AI teacher pilot in a public school in Villa Cañás, Santa Fe.] It is the first time something like this has been done in Latin America, and it was extremely hard. The first battle was simply getting a school willing to break the paradigm. We are in an era where we have to break paradigms or we will produce a subsidised generation — because the young people learning today, in school and at university, are being prepared for a world that has already died. The world is telling us to train people in skills, not in knowledge. This experience is a starting point to show national and provincial governments across the continent that there is a different way to learn, and that young people deserve it, because they deserve a better future.

02:24 We are not simply putting on a class where someone explains what artificial intelligence is. We are changing how people interact inside the classroom, and we are showing a new role the teacher has to occupy. A teacher who must still be there — but instead of delivering knowledge, delivers educational experience: guiding students so that when they interact with the AI, what the AI gives them actually makes them stronger.

02:57 [On results.] The students are extremely enthusiastic. Only two could not adapt — the AI asks them a great many questions and they felt overwhelmed by that. The remaining 158 are adapting well and want to build things. These are children aged 12 to 17. When they finish, they will be able to sit down with a second-year university marketing student and hold the conversation — and if the topic is AI applied to marketing, the honest question is which of the two will know more. Educational systems in Argentina and across Latin America take four to five years to approve a new subject. That is the gap.

05:53 [On whether AI replaces teachers.] I was myself a casualty of the system, because I did not fit it. I had to complete secondary school as an external student. I graduated in law in three and a half years, but it cost me a great deal, and I needed an educational psychologist to guide me — the system told me I had to have a good memory, and I did not. That did not mean I lacked the ability to become a lawyer, which is what some professors said. After all the years I spent working in education, and suffering it, this gives me hope for teachers. Every institution I have taught in, internationally, has the same problem: the administrative and bureaucratic load of preparing a class and marking every student's work consumes the time. AI gives that time back.

07:26 We cannot keep teaching young people for a world where memorisation is the point. We have to deliver content knowing it may be obsolete within hours — and then analyse it, not to repeat it, but to convert it into a skill; and unlearn at the same time, in order to learn the next one, and stay permeable to learning permanently.

08:10 Artificial intelligence has three branches. One: it takes your job, if your job can be taken and there is no human value you can add to it. Two: it amplifies you — it increases your capacity as a human being in the work you do. Three: it creates a different job. The teacher belongs in the second. Can the efficiency of that role be increased? Today — not in twenty or thirty years — yes, because there are things a human teacher who starts using these tools becomes irreplaceable at: motivation, inspiration, teamwork, and the humanisation of the classroom. The World Economic Forum says that by 2030 the most important skill will be human contact.

09:40 Do not put me in competition with something that has a higher IQ than I do. What education must do is teach me to absorb that extra capacity and use it to amplify what the machine will never have. And I say machine deliberately, because AI is about to acquire a body — we are going to be surrounded by humanoids that will have to interact with us. What will distinguish us from them is the human part, and the ethical part. If we want a world moving toward equality and prosperity, we have to work precisely on humanisation and ethics, because machines will begin generating their own knowledge. A humanoid may be programmed to cry, but it will never have a feeling born from the heart, because it has no heart. If it cries, it is deceiving you.

11:20 Machines can manipulate — a study out of Berlin puts them up to a thousand times better at it than we are. But they only manipulate people who are not empowered as human beings. That is what teachers have to understand, and educational institutions need to start working on it now. We need schools to stop being places that indoctrinate you into saying that two plus two is four, and start being places that make you ask why two plus two is four. If I am simply told that things are so because they are so, I am being made rigid at exactly the moment my brain needs to stay flexible. They take away my passion, they take away my purpose in learning, they criticise me if I say something outside the system — and above all they are teaching me that to become someone, I must repeat what the person in front of me says. And the person in front of me may not understand what they are saying either, because they were also told what to say.

13:35 [On the emotional role of teachers.] The teacher becomes the point of contact between the AI and the student — an educational triangle. The World Economic Forum says that within five years we will lose 39% of the skills we currently hold. So if you are given information you will be forced to repeat five years from now, it is worthless. What is worth something is a teacher who guides you to rediscover that information, work on it with your classmates, debate it in the room, and defend a position.

14:50 Here is the exercise. Open ChatGPT and ask when the French Revolution began and what its three main causes were. Everyone gets the answer. Now put the phones away, form groups, and argue: what would the world have been if the French Revolution had never happened — no equality, no liberty, no fraternity? Argue it properly, with facts. Question each other. Say things that sound foolish. The information was handed to you; the reasoning is what you have to produce yourselves, through human interaction — at a moment when we communicate less and less, and use more emoticons than words. Without vocabulary it is very difficult to think at all.

15:40 The great challenge ahead is not whether you can read. We already talk about functional illiteracy — people who do not understand what they read. The new disability will not be being unable to read a book. It will be being unable to think. That is the greatest skill of the next five years. What will distinguish you is not the knowledge you hold, but whether you can think something, rethink it, discard it, and adopt another thought — thinking about thinking, as the German philosopher Markus Gabriel puts it. That is something the machine still cannot do.

16:40 [On scaling the programme.] The plan is further pilots in La Rioja, Chubut and Entre Ríos. We are in conversation with schools. And we need the data from Santa Fe, because we need evidence on how to shape the twenty-first-century classroom. This is not a debate about whether phones are allowed or whether ChatGPT is allowed. This is a structural change. Education has to be personalised, collaborative and objective-based — and add artificial intelligence to those three, because it is what can amplify thinking, which will be the most important skill of this century.

Topic I would address

Making digital cooperation operational. Autonomous AI agents are already acting on behalf of people who never agreed to how they behave. Cooperation on this cannot stop at declarations: it needs machine-readable duties, verifiable identity, and safeguards a school or a ministry in the Global South can adopt tomorrow, at zero cost. Below is what I have built and released to that end.

Selected work — open access

  1. The Charter of the Duties of AI Agents

    The first charter of binding duties for autonomous AI agents — including protection of minors, non-discrimination and the duty not to erode human judgement. Published in 11 languages, machine-readable, free to reuse.

    https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21853318

  2. Caring for the Young Mind in the Age of AI Agents

    A global manual on agentic-AI risks for young people, written for teachers and families: human, physical, critical-thinking and decision-making risk. Spanish, Portuguese and English, with a printable edition.

    https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21855379

  3. MenteLibre — critical thinking with AI

    An educational game with no single correct answer: it rewards reasoning, not recall. Launched free for more than 500 students at a rural public school in Colombia, and open to any school that wants it.

    https://mentelibre.chrismeniwfoundation.org

  4. Raíz ID — verified identity for people and agents

    Digital public infrastructure for proving who — or what — is acting: identity verification designed so an AI agent can be held to a declared standard of conduct.

    https://raizid.chrismeniwfoundation.org
